## What to expect

No forecast here would be honest. There is no keyword data, no existing audience measured, and no prior launch to compare with. What the evidence supports: buyers vet under five suppliers and decide mostly from the website; engineers find LinkedIn useful but do not live there; the people most likely to send work in the first month are people who already know the owner. So the plan spends its first week on people who know him, and the rest on building a short, hand-picked list. Judge it at day 30 by counts you can see: CAD uploads on the quote tool, firm-quote requests, phone calls, orders, and names collected.

### Post 2 — day 8 — plastic moves

No photo needed. A photo of a long UHMW or HDPE part on the bench with a tape on it would help.

> A 30-inch HDPE panel that fits its steel frame at 72 °F is about 0.056" longer at 100 °F. The steel frame grew 0.005". The panel has nowhere to go, so it buckles.
>
> That example is from a Curbell Plastics paper on tolerances, and I think about it whenever a print comes in with ±0.005" on a two-foot polyethylene part.
>
> Plastic isn't soft aluminum.
>
> HDPE grows about ten times as much as steel for the same temperature change. Nylon takes on water and grows with it, around 2% in length if it's soaked. And most plastics relax after they're cut, so a part that measures right on the machine can measure different the next morning.
>
> None of that is a reason to avoid plastic. It's a reason to do four things:
>
> Tell the shop where the part lives. Hot, wet, washdown, outdoors.
> Put the tight tolerance on the one feature that needs it.
> Pick acetal when a dimension matters and UHMW when wear matters.
> Let the parts sit before anyone measures them.
>
> If you've got a plastic part that keeps coming in wrong, send me the print. Sometimes the problem is the material or the tolerance, not the shop that cut it.

Sources for the numbers: Curbell Plastics, *Dimensional Tolerance Challenges with Plastic Parts* (2023): HDPE 6.7 vs. 1020 steel 0.65 ×10⁻⁵ in/in/°F; panel 29.97" → 30.026"; frame 30.000" → 30.005"; nylon 6 +2% length at saturation. Depends on: the owner agreeing this matches his experience. No shop claims.

### Post 4 — day 17 — what the router is for

Photo: the HDPE trays or another routed, pocketed part. [CONFIRM: photo cleared, audit C25]

> These are 3/4" HDPE, cut from sheet on the router. [CONFIRM: caption matches the photo] Outside profile, pockets, holes and broken edges, all in one setup.
>
> A lot of flat plastic parts get made the expensive way.
>
> If your part is flat, an inch thick or less, and has pockets or counterbores, it belongs on a router. Milling it from a block means paying to turn most of the material into chips. A laser or a waterjet cuts all the way through; it can't cut a pocket partway.
>
> A router does the profile, the pockets and the chamfers in one pass over the sheet, and the price drops fast when the quantity fills a whole sheet.
>
> You can check this yourself. Upload the part at straightedgemfg.com and switch the process between machining and routing. The price changes on the screen. If routing is cheaper, that's the one to buy, and the quote says so.
>
> Bed is 59" × 96". Plastic from 1/16" to 1" thick. [CONFIRM: thickness range]

Depends on: photo clearance, router facts (audit A43, A44). The process switch and live re-price are verified in the app.

### Post 5 — day 22 — what plastic will hold

No photo needed. A photo of parts on the surface plate would fit, if the plate is in the shop.

> An engineer on Eng-Tips once asked whether the flatness he wanted on a plastic plate was "asking too much." The most honest reply in the thread was: it depends.
>
> Here is what it depends on in my shop.
>
> Acetal (Delrin): [CONFIRM: ±0.005" standard; ±0.002" on a feature you call out]. It's the plastic to pick when a dimension matters.
>
> Nylon: [CONFIRM]. I cut it dry and tell you the size as shipped, because it grows when it takes on moisture.
>
> UHMW and HDPE: [CONFIRM: ±0.010"]. On anything long, tell me the temperature it runs at. They move with the weather.
>
> PTFE: wide. It creeps under its own clamping.
>
> PEEK: [CONFIRM]. Holds size well, and the stock costs enough that I'd rather talk before I cut.
>
> Two things lower the price of almost any plastic part. Put the tight number only on the feature that needs it. And tell me what the part does, because the right material often fixes what a tighter tolerance can't.
>
> If a print asks for something the material won't keep, I'll say so before I cut it.

Depends on: the per-material tolerance table (audit C11). Do not post until every bracket is filled with a number the owner will be held to. Source for the quote: Eng-Tips thread "Flatness" (TheMotionGuy), https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/flatness.276245.
